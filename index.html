Prologue

Before It All

March — July 2022
offline

“Every journey has a version of you that didn't know what was coming.”

In March 2022 I was still a college student, sitting through interview rounds for a company I mostly knew by name. It was a campus placement. It felt less like choosing a career and more like a door opening while I happened to be standing near it.

In April the Letter of Intent arrived. Then on 21 June 2022 came the offer letter, the real thing, with my name on it. I was very happy. I don't think I can put it more precisely than that, and I don't think I need to.

12 July 2022.
The laptop arrived ten days before I did.

On 12 July a laptop showed up at my door. It's a strange thing to feel emotional about a machine, but it was the first piece of DXC that was physically mine. The job was no longer a letter. It was something I could switch on.

Chapter I

Day One

22 July — August 2022
offline

I joined DXC Technology on Friday, 22 July 2022. On paper my work location was Chennai. In practice it was wherever my laptop was.

The offer letter said we would work from home for as long as COVID-19 cases continued. Nobody, least of all me, guessed that the arrangement would outlast the reason for it.

Four years at DXC. Not a single day in the office.

It's a good deal, and I know it. But there's a small, quiet gap in this story where a first walk into the Chennai office should have been: a badge swipe, a desk, a canteen. I never got to have that moment. The whole journey happened through a screen, and somehow it still felt like a place.

The first weeks were Wintel training, delivered by an external vendor. On 30 August 2022 I sent a short email with a two-word subject: Training Completed. Then I was standing at the edge of the real work, waiting.

Chapter II

The Phone Call

September — December 2022
available

“The hardest part of starting is the part before you start.”

After training came the waiting, the strange in-between time of being employed but not yet assigned. In mid-September there was a business handover call for our batch. Then, in the second week of September, my phone rang.

It was a team lead. He was adding me to the BP (British Petroleum) project. One phone call, and suddenly I had a client, a team and a name to attach to my work.

A global energy company with servers everywhere: refineries, terminals, offshore rigs. My first client, and my home for the next two years.

By October I was going through BP account onboarding: security training, access requests, the many small gates you pass through before a client trusts you with its servers. Around the same time I was asked to keep an eye on the emails and tasks for an Aon project. I never actually worked on Aon, but for a couple of months I watched its inbox, learning the rhythm of how requests arrive, pile up and get picked up.

On 22 September the first DXC Champs winners list of my career landed in my inbox. I was two months in, and I opened it anyway. It was the first of ten lists I would open.

On 2 December 2022, my first incident landed in my name: INC25674986, for the BP Wintel team. One ticket. It sounds like nothing. It was everything. It was the moment I stopped being a trainee who had read about the work and became someone who had done it.

Ten days later, on 12 December, I sent my first shift Open Report. There would be 147 of them, and they were the start of a habit that would run for the rest of my DXC life.

I didn't know what a Wintel server was. Then I worked my first incident on one.
Chapter III

Learning to Speak Wintel

January — June 2023
busy

“A new language, one ticket at a time.”

My email signature said ITO Service Delivery Consultant – BP Wintel/VMware Account, and the job was exactly that: Wintel troubleshooting across BP's worldwide estate. Servers that were “possibly down”. C drives running out of space at refineries in Whiting and Cherry Point. Failed fans and drive arrays chased with hardware vendors. Access requests, file shares, vulnerabilities, certificates.

And every month, the patching: copying the month's KBs to isolated PIN and PCN servers and patching them by hand, because some of the most important machines in the world can't just phone home for updates.

On 20 February 2023 I wrote my first shift handover. It would not be my last, not by a long way.

Some requests became rituals. A bp team in Indonesia sent housekeeping tasks for its OpenText servers almost every week, and their reply always began the same way. I counted seventy of them between November 2022 and July 2023.

“Hi Kumar, thank you so much for your support!”bp Indonesia · seventy times over2022 – 2023

In March a teammate pinged me a simple question on Teams: how many UCMS cases had I raised that financial year? UCMS was DXC's Use Case Management System, where automation ideas went to become real. The honest answer was none. I filed that away.

BP was starting to leave its data centres, and the first exit I watched up close was Bochum, closed in the spring of 2023. I didn't know it yet, but exits would become my speciality.

Not every relationship was warm. There were a couple of colleagues on the BP Wintel team I didn't particularly like, and I'm fairly sure the feeling was mutual. That's work too. You don't have to be friends with everyone to get the job done together.

Chapter IV

July 2023 — March 2024
dnd

“The most hectic months of my life were also the most fun. I don't think that's a coincidence.”

On 3 July 2023 I moved to the BP BDC team. Here the work was change requests: planned, approved, scheduled and executed changes, each one a small promise that nothing would break.

Chapter V

The Rig at the Edge of the Map

April — May 2024
meeting

“The proudest exit was the smallest one on the map.”

In April 2024 the BP Wintel and BP BDC teams merged, and I started working for both. BDC put me in touch with more colleagues and stakeholders than ever before. The job demanded it, and I learned to speak to all of them.

A new name enters · Apr 2024

Rolls-Royce

Wintel support alongside BP, onboarded at the end of April 2024. A second client in the same inbox, until the work ended with the BP handover.

Wintel
Spring 2024.
My Sent Items stopped at 23 on one particular day. The shared mailboxes would tell a longer story. Let's just call it “a lot”.

Of all the exits, the one I'm proudest of is the smallest on a map: the Ensco-106 drilling rig, offshore Papua in Indonesia. In May 2024 it was decommissioned completely. That meant the whole site, down to the satellite dome and the last Starlink link.

The project plan listed every server in sequence, with a technical lead and a delegate for each. For the BP BDC team, the delegate was me. My part was the rig's legacy file server: wiping its disks, asking for an exception to skip the usual cooling-off period, and coordinating with the onsite crew so they could shred the disks and unrack the hardware before the boats left.

On a Friday evening, when most of us were heading into the weekend, we were all on a call confirming our parts so the crew could keep working on the rig through Saturday and Sunday. The team was spread across Papua, Jakarta, India, Sunbury, South Africa and Houston, and it all held together through one Teams group chat.

On 13 May the project manager sent the closing note. It thanked all of us for responding “with a sense of urgency for the Business”, and ended like this:

You should all be proud of what we've delivered in such a demanding timeline.

I was. I still am.

“Thank you for the information and quick response.”bp partnerMay 2024
Chapter VI

Sixteen Hours

July 2024
busy

“Some of the work you do will never have your name on it. Do it anyway.”

In July 2024 the CrowdStrike update went out and the world's Windows machines went down with it. For the Wintel team, that meant servers falling over one after another.

I joined the bridge call. Then I stayed on it with my colleagues, working server by server across an estate of more than two hundred, for sixteen hours, until the issue was sorted.

Each square: one hour on the bridge

On 22 July the leadership note arrived. The client had written in to say thank you.

“There are times when DXC shines incredibly bright!”DXC leadership · after CrowdStrike22 July 2024

When the appreciation was shared, it named everyone involved. Everyone except me. Four days later, on 26 July, I was still at it, sending round the spreadsheet of incident tickets that hadn't closed on their own so the last servers could be cleaned up.

Sixteen hours on the bridge. My name wasn't on the thank-you mail.

That was sad, and I won't pretend otherwise. But the servers came back up. The people on that call know who was there. And now, so does this book.

Chapter VIII

A Second Language

December 2024 — May 2025
brb

“I had just become fluent in servers. Now I had to learn people.”

Wintel had been about machines. UAA was about people: who could log in, to what, and for how long. New joiners, leavers, privileged accounts, shared folders, expired passwords at awkward hours. The tickets were smaller, but every one of them had a person waiting on the other end.

In February 2025 the UCMS question from two years earlier finally got a better answer. Two of my automation cases went through the pipeline: UCMS #88094 and UCMS #88127. After a test call with the automation team, I sent both closure confirmations the same week, with the same line: “the script is working fine as expected.”

“Thank you very much Nikhil, the password works. We appreciate your quick response on this.”Grupo Bimbo service partnerMarch 2025

In April 2025 I went back to class for a three-day PowerShell course. The scripts were about to get bigger.

Chapter X

Writing It Down

June — December 2025
away

“If I did it twice, I scripted it. If someone else had to do it, I wrote it down.”

On 19 June 2025 an email arrived about UCMS #88095, a case filed under my name for the Vale account: a PowerShell script to create local users and add them to the administrator group. The problem, as I described it, was that doing this by hand across many servers was “time-consuming and error-prone.” The solution takes a server list and does the whole lot in one go.

The case changed hands once that same week, went through a requirement-gathering call, and on 10 July 2025 reached deployment, with a handbook and a read-me attached. I signed it off: “Please proceed to close the UCMS case.”

Chapter XI

The Final Year

January — November 2026
busy

“The last year is when you finally know how everything works.”

For Grupo Bimbo's RBAC work, someone had to keep track of when vendor accounts would expire. It was a manual, error-prone check that nobody enjoyed. So on 26 June 2026 I wrote a PowerShell script to do it, and sent it to the whole Brazil UAA team with step-by-step instructions: open the AD module, paste, press Enter. No changes are needed. The script works as-is for everyone.

It sorted every account into Active, Expiring Soon, Expired or Never Expires, and exported the lot to a CSV.

# Account expiry report (simplified; real paths removed)
$Today    = Get-Date
$Accounts = Get-ADUser -SearchBase $TargetOU -Filter * -Properties AccountExpirationDate

$Accounts | ForEach-Object {
    $exp = $_.AccountExpirationDate
    $status = if (-not $exp)                 { "Never Expires" }
              elseif ($exp -lt $Today)          { "Expired" }
              elseif ($exp -lt $Today.AddDays(30)) { "Expiring Soon" }
              else                              { "Active" }
    [PSCustomObject]@{ Name = $_.Name; Expires = $exp; Status = $status }
} | Export-Csv ".\Account_Expiry.csv" -NoTypeInformation

I never shared a desk with any of them. I never shared a lunch table. And yet some of the people I worked with at DXC know me better than people I've met in person.

It started with a team lead's phone call in September 2022 and the BP Wintel crew who taught me what “possibly down” really meant. It grew in BDC, where every decommission meant a new name on the thread: a refinery engineer, a rig crew, a project manager in Jakarta. And it settled into the UAA India team, where the chat never really went quiet. Teammates picked up my handovers at midnight, and I picked up theirs.

❦ ❦ ❦To the many whose names this page has no room for: the people I had good times and long conversations with, the friends from the BP years who have since moved on, and everyone who turned a laptop screen into an office. You are written in the margins of every chapter. A book has only so many pages. Gratitude keeps no such count.

Epilogue

The Last Page

Monday · 30 November 2026

When I joined, I knew every one of these words. In English. Then IT gave each of them a second meaning, and for a while I was quietly translating in my head.

The wordWhat I thought it meantWhat it meant at DXC
ServiceNowAn impatient customerMuscle memory
WintelA typo for IntelWindows on Intel. Home turf.
Possibly downHow I felt on Monday morningsA server that stopped answering pings
TicketSomething for a trainINC25674986, the first of many
Change requestAsking someone to changePlanned, approved, windowed. Hundreds.
IncidentSomething that makes the newsP1 to P4. Handled.
PatchingWhat you do to old jeansA monthly KB ritual
BridgeSomething over a riverSixteen hours, one July
DecommissionRetiring a warshipWipe, shred, unrack. Even a rig.
HandoverPassing a parcelMidnight's most important email

Now they're the backbone of how I speak professionally, and of my career. The translation runs the other way these days: I hear “bridge” and think of a call.

I wrote my first shift handover in February 2023, and stopped counting long ago. Somewhere in between, “handover” stopped being a task and became a rhythm: tell the next person what's burning, what's done, and what to watch.

But the biggest thing I'm taking with me isn't a skill. It's a different kind of confidence. The student who sat through interviews in March 2022 had plenty of the first kind: the belief that he could walk into anything and figure it out. What he didn't have yet was the kind that only shows up when things go wrong.

It's the confidence of a “possibly down” alert on a night shift, when the next move is mine, and knowing what to check first. Of sixteen hours on a bridge while servers fall over one after another, and staying steady. Of asking for the cooling-off period to be skipped because a rig is about to be dismantled, and being sure it's the right call. Of a change window ticking down, a thread full of people waiting, and trusting my own judgement enough to say go, or stop.

Nobody hands you that. It built up one incident, one change window, one handover at a time, until pressure stopped feeling like panic and started feeling like focus.

That's what I'm taking with me.
